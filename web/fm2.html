Partial gravity · Flammability of Materials on the Moon (FM2)

The Moon is not halfway between orbit and Earth

It is natural to assume that a material's fire behaviour in lunar gravity falls somewhere between its behaviour in microgravity and on Earth. The published data say otherwise, and NASA's FM2 mission is designed to test it on the Moon itself.

Why two endpoints do not bracket the middle

A flame on a solid needs a steady supply of oxygen, and it must keep enough of its own heat to keep vaporising fuel. The air flow past the flame controls both.

  • Too little flow (still air in microgravity): oxygen arrives only by diffusion and the flame loses heat faster than it makes it. This is the quenching branch.
  • Too much flow (strong buoyant plume on Earth, or a fast fan): the gases are swept away before they finish reacting. This is the blowoff branch.
  • In between, a gentle flow feeds the flame without blowing it out, so it can survive at a lower oxygen level.

On Earth, buoyancy drives a fast upward flow. In lunar or Martian gravity that buoyant flow is weaker, which can place a flame nearer the low point of the curve. So the hardest-to-extinguish condition can sit between microgravity and 1g, not at either end.

Test ducts and burners impose their own flow. That flow is not the same as cabin ventilation, so limits measured in a duct should not be read as limits for a real cabin.
